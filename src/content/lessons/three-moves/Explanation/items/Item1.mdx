We can only reach spot 100 from spot 99, 98, and 97.
If we can figure out the number of ways to reach (end - 1 jump), (end - 2 jumps), and (end - 3 jumps),
we will automatically know the total ways to reach the destination.

But then for spot 99, 98, and 97, they can only be reached from 1 to 3 spots prior to each of them respectively since these are the only moves we make from these locations.

We can see that we have subproblems forming.
We're able to find the total number of ways to reach any spot on the board if we know the total number of ways to reach the prior 3 spots since the steps we can take from any location is 1, 2, or 3 hops.

Let's visualize this using our sample input/output where we had a board length of 4:

<Image src="/images/three-moves-example.png" alt="A small board lists sequences of jumps of length one, two, or three that reach each position." width={1736} height={1162} />

- There is 1 path to reach the final spot by going through spot 1
- There are 2 paths to reach the final spot by going through spot 2
- There are 4 paths to reach the final spot by going through spot 3

So since we reach spot 1 once and then do a 3-jump, this is 1 path to the final spot.
We reach spot 2 twice and do a 2-jump both times which is 2 paths to the final spot.
We reach spot 3 four times and do a 1-jump which is 4 paths to the final spot.

If we count the number of ways to the final spot from these 3 prior spots, we see it is our solution of 7.
There are 7 total unique paths to reach spot 4 by using a 1-jump, 2-jumps, and 3-jumps from the three previous spots.

<Image src="/images/sum-of-moves.png" alt="The paths to a position are combined from paths ending one, two, and three spaces earlier." width={1906} height={1168} />

Also notice that we're only counting the ways to reach the final spot and not any of the spots in between.
When we found the number of ways to reach spot 2, this already accounted for the fact that we had a path `1,1` which went through spot 1.
When we looked at the paths to reach spot 3, this already accounted for the ways to reach spot 3 by going through both spots 1 and 2.
We'll see in a moment, this is formally defined because they were already accounted for when solving each prior spot's subproblem.

There is a path to the final spot through the 3 prior spots.
So if we sum the total number of ways to reach the 3 prior spots on the board,
this produces the combination of total unique paths to reach the final spot.

And this is true for _any_ board length.
